The problem

A structural voxel is assembled from flat panels that slot together. On the ground a person holds panels while others are inserted. In orbit there is no person, so the jig has to do the holding, the joint has to tolerate imperfect alignment, and every insertion motion should look the same so the robot program stays simple.

What I did

  • Framed the design space on three axes — jig location (internal core / external cradle / hybrid), panels per insertion (one at a time / two-panel seed / chunked), and degrees of freedom (static fixture / rotating index / collapsing jig) — then generated concepts against that grid instead of jumping to a favorite.
  • Compared four candidates: petal-fold turntable, horizontal "rotisserie" axle, internal retractable jack, and external nest on a turntable. Scored each on competitive performance, simplicity (fewest DOF), and compatibility (motions needing the least sensing feedback).
  • Modeled the jig top in Creo — dedicated top-panel clamps, side-panel clamps, and panel cutouts — and worked through capture mechanisms: torsion-spring hooks with a released stopper, over-center toggle clamps, two-state spring latches, and weight-actuated gates.
  • Pushed for one-way-only joints with chamfered lead-ins so a misaligned panel fails to insert rather than jamming halfway. Inserting wrong is worse than not inserting at all.
  • Built Blender storyboards from imported CAD to show each insertion sequence frame by frame — much faster than arguing about it verbally.
  • Rebuilt a robotic end effector from 3D-printed parts to metal: tapped M3/M4 threads, installed helicoils, hand-filed components to fit, and kept a running catalog of which replacement parts held tolerance and which didn't.

What testing changed

  • Mounted a completed voxel on a stand, cut cardboard-and-Velcro panel mockups, and hand-jogged a UR3e through the planned insertion. Cheap, and it broke several assumptions immediately.
  • Found unreachable poses inside the 500 mm working envelope, and confirmed the arm will take its own path unless waypoints are set explicitly.
  • Panels collided with the arm body — the end effector needed more standoff from the platform, and the jig height had to land in a narrow band: high enough that the arm isn't folded on itself, low enough to stay in reach.

The problem

The team was doing one-off drone builds with no shared way to compare propulsion options. This test stand allowed them to make data-driven decisions and compare/benchmark motor options.

Building the test article

  • Spliced and heat-shrunk motor leads for clean routing; soldered ESC and header pins into the flight controller.
  • Ran a 10 AWG main power path to keep voltage drop off the measurement, terminated with XT60 connectors so battery swaps were quick and consistent.
  • Threaded every signal and power cable through a single centralized base pass-through — no exposed wiring in the prop wash, no run-to-run variation from cable dress.

The protocol

  • Throttle sweep from 0% to 100% in 10% steps with a 3-second settle at each point, so readings are taken at steady state rather than mid-transient.
  • Anchored selection to mission profile: 5-inch builds tuned for agility, 10-inch for endurance. Those want different points on the same curve.
  • Defined a target thrust point from all-up weight and the mission's thrust-to-weight ratio, then matched motors by peak efficiency percentage and props by highest N/W output at that specific thrust — not at max throttle, where nothing actually flies.

The system

Lumius is commercializing 3D diffractive acoustic tomography — a method that gets volumetric imaging out of an off-the-shelf linear array transducer by diffracting the acoustic field through a single adjustable slit. It sidesteps the usual matrix-array tradeoff, where shrinking elements to densify the sampled field of view costs you detection sensitivity. The probe runs two synchronized modes: photoacoustic, using multi-wavelength laser illumination, and ultrasound, using a plane wave diffracted by the slit.

Why the enclosure is hard

  • Acoustic coupling — a trapped air bubble blocks ultrasound outright, so the geometry has to evacuate air, not just contain fluid.
  • Leak prevention — escaping fluid means either wet electronics or fluid on a patient.
  • Biocompatibility — the face touching skin has to be a medical-grade material.
  • Optical transparency — the photoacoustic mode fires lasers through the same window the ultrasound passes through.
  • A moving part inside the seal — the diffractive slit has to keep sliding on its track while submerged, which rules out most of the easy sealing answers.

What I designed

  • Modeled the enclosure in Fusion 360, importing McMaster-Carr models of the actual fasteners and hardware so fits were checked against real parts rather than nominal dimensions.
  • Worked out the internal architecture: a recessed external pocket for a Xeryon XLA-1-50 piezo actuator, a wall thinned to 1.0 mm across the actuator’s travel to maximize magnetic flux without losing stiffness, a longitudinal sled track for the slit mount, and internal bosses carrying press-fit 3 mm stainless guide rails to keep the sled stable.
  • Tapered the patient-contact face down to a 0.5 mm acoustic window — thin enough to pass sound, thick enough to survive handling — and filleted external vertical edges 5 mm for grip.
  • Evaluated two ways to drive the slit through a sealed wall: magnetic coupling and a rubber accordion bellows. Both were confirmed viable; the magnetic approach kept losing consistent contact across the intervening membrane, which became the open issue to solve.
  • Consulted Duke acoustics faculty on whether candidate geometries would hold acoustic coupling before committing to one.

The hygiene problem, and the business answer

Between patients the probe has to be cleaned, but the fluid volume is part of the acoustic path — you can’t drain and refill it in a clinic. I proposed a removable bottom cartridge: the patient-contact face becomes a replaceable consumable, so the fluid is never changed and hygiene is handled by swapping a part. That doubles as a razor-and-blades revenue path — sell the probe once, sell cartridges repeatedly. Design review also moved the tilt to the side and added a control button for one-handed operation.

Side investigation — benchmarking text-to-CAD tools

  • Evaluated Zoo, Adam, and Aurorin against an escalating prompt ladder to find where generative CAD stops being useful.
  • The ladder: a 50 mm cube with a centered through-hole and fillets (~5 min on Zoo’s fast mode) → a 100 × 100 × 10 mm plate with four M4 clearance holes → a plate with a raised boss carrying three M3 tapped holes on a 20 mm bolt circle, exported as solid B-Rep STEP → the full parametric probe enclosure.
  • Zoo behaved like a real CAD environment — manual editing after generation, a code-level representation of features, broad export options — and took about 20 minutes on the full enclosure prompt. Adam was faster to learn but offered no manual editing and exported only STEP and SCAD; it could not handle the enclosure prompt at all.
  • The breaking point across every tool was threaded and patterned features. Prismatic geometry generated fine; tapped holes on a bolt circle did not.

The problem

What we built

  • Built a custom sensing circuit and integrated Arduino firmware to trigger an alert when the level leaves the safe band.
  • CAD-modeled and fabricated a wooden enclosure, chosen for local repairability over a printed or machined housing.
