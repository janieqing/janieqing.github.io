TLT end-effector rebuild

Disassembled and rebuilt two robotic end-effectors, swapping 3D-printed components for machined metal to improve thermal vacuum durability. Tolerances did not carry over, so most parts needed hand adjustment to fit.

  • Metal electronics enclosures (×2): fabricated and fitted: filed the edges and lid so it snapped in and sat flush, opened up the lid holes to match the base, and swapped the screws mounting it to the metal center hub.
  • Center gear box / hub (×2): replaced internal components with metal, re-tapped and resoldered the limit switch components, straightened a bent center rod, and turned holes into slots so the electronics box screws would fit.
  • Motor and clamp assemblies (×4): all printed parts swapped for metal, screws shortened from M2.5×8 to M2.5×6, and specific clamp holes re-tapped M3.
  • Rivet gun plates (×4): metal camera mounts, with M4 helicoils installed in the base plate to adapt to the smaller hole size on the new mounts.
  • Debugged motor-driven telescoping rods where only one side extended. Traced it to that side sitting too close to the end and catching on the limit switch.

Roughly 12 parts replaced per end-effector, improving thermal vacuum readiness from 64% to 95%.

Autonomous unit-cell assembly

Robots already do voxel-to-voxel assembly of lattice structures. Building each individual voxel is still done by hand. Someone holds the panels while the rest get inserted. My project was making that step robotic so the process scales.

  • Defined a UR3e join sequence to autonomously assemble six panels into a modular cuboctahedron unit with repeatable alignment.
  • Modeled and visualized assembly configurations and approaches in Creo and Blender, storyboarding each insertion step frame by frame.
  • Mapped the design space on three axes: jig location (internal core / external cradle / hybrid), panels per insertion, and degrees of freedom. Compared four concepts against it: petal-fold turntable, horizontal rotisserie axle, internal retractable jack, and external nest on a turntable.
  • Modeled the jig top with top-panel clamps, side-panel clamps, and panel cutouts. Worked through capture mechanisms including torsion-spring hooks, over-center toggle clamps, two-state spring latches, and weight-actuated gates.
  • Co-designed the panel joint with the intern designing the panels, and proposed male/female joints over the node-centered snowflake joints, with lead-in slots so alignment clearance and error margins were larger during assembly.
  • Pushed for joints that only go together one way. A panel that fails to insert is recoverable; one that inserts wrong is not.

Chosen approach and insertion order

Narrowed to upside-down construction: the UR3e sits at the center base and builds top-down, pressing each panel into the jig, with a retractable clamp mechanism releasing the finished voxel. It keeps the robot from having to extend or move far, and the voxel comes out cleanly once complete.

  • Sequential insertion: top plate, then side plates one at a time around the voxel, then the bottom. Each panel braces the next, the robot motion is identical for every side panel, and the jig stays simple. Downside is that errors stack as you go around the perimeter, and the slide-up motion causes joint connection problems.
  • Mirrored insertion: top plate, then two mirrored side plates from below, then the last two pressed in flat from the side, then the bottom. Four points of contact on the final panels and less jig complexity, but it needs two different insertion motions and pushes the difficulty into the clamping mechanism.
  • Proposed a latch and two-state spring clamp as the primary mechanism: the strut presses the mechanism down and the latch closes over it, so the robot does not need extra attachments on the panels.

Prototype testing

  • Mounted a completed voxel to a stand, used cardboard cutouts with Velcro as panel prototypes, and manipulated the UR3e through the planned insertion.
  • Found unreachable poses inside the 500 mm working envelope. The arm takes its own path unless waypoints are set explicitly.
  • Panels collided with the arm body, so the end effector needed more standoff, and jig height had to sit in a narrow band, high enough that the arm isn't folded on itself and low enough to stay in reach.

Propulsion characterization

My work was aimed at moving the team from isolated one-off drone builds toward a repeatable, data-driven testing and assembly setup. The team needed a proper testing space to find the optimal propulsion configuration for 5- and 10-inch builds, so I built and wired the thrust stand and wrote the protocol, then analyzed efficiency, load response, and performance across 6 motor and propeller configurations.

Build

  • Spliced and heat-shrunk motor leads; soldered ESC and header pins into the flight controller.
  • Ran a 10 AWG main power path to keep voltage drop out of the measurement, terminated with XT60 so battery swaps stay consistent.
  • Routed every signal and power cable through one centralized base pass-through, so nothing was exposed in the prop wash, no run-to-run variation from cable dress.

Protocol

  • Throttle sweep 0% to 100% in 10% steps, 3-second settle at each point so readings are at steady state.
  • Selection tied to mission profile: 5-inch builds for agility, 10-inch for endurance.
  • Set a target thrust point from all-up weight and thrust-to-weight ratio, then picked motors by peak efficiency and props by highest N/W at that thrust, not at max throttle.

The system

Lumius is building 3D diffractive acoustic tomography, which gets 3D imaging from an off-the-shelf linear array transducer by diffracting the acoustic field through a single adjustable slit. This avoids the matrix-array tradeoff, where shrinking elements for a denser sampled field of view costs detection sensitivity. The probe runs two modes: photoacoustic with multi-wavelength laser illumination, and ultrasound with a plane wave diffracted by the slit.

Leak-proof probe enclosure

Design constraints

  • Acoustic coupling: air bubbles block ultrasound, so the geometry has to clear air, not just hold fluid.
  • Leak prevention: fluid can't reach the electronics or the patient.
  • Biocompatibility: the face touching skin has to be medical-grade.
  • Transparency: the photoacoustic mode fires lasers through the same window.
  • Moving part inside the seal: the imaging window still has to slide while submerged.

What I did

  • Modeled the housing and a sliding magnetic leak-proof imaging window in Fusion 360, with McMaster-Carr models of the actual hardware inserted so fits were checked against real parts.
  • Laid out the internals: recessed external pocket for the piezo actuator, thinned wall across the actuator travel to maximize magnetic flux without losing stiffness, longitudinal sled track for the window mount, internal bosses with press-fit stainless guide rails for sled stability.
  • Tapered the patient-contact face down to a thin acoustic window and filleted the external vertical edges for grip.
  • Compared magnetic coupling against a rubber accordion bellows for driving the window through a sealed wall. Went with magnetic and worked on keeping consistent contact across the membrane.
  • Checked acoustic coupling on candidate geometries with Duke acoustics faculty before committing.
  • Prepared a prototype for the team's YC interview.

Hygiene

The probe has to be cleaned between patients, but the fluid is part of the acoustic path so you can't drain and refill it in a clinic. I proposed a removable bottom cartridge, where the patient-contact face becomes a replaceable part, so the fluid never gets changed.

Text-to-CAD tool evaluation
  • Compared Zoo, Adam, and Aurorin on the same prompts to find where generative CAD stops being useful.
  • Prompt ladder: 50 mm cube with a centered through-hole and fillets (~5 min on Zoo fast mode) → 100 × 100 × 10 mm plate with four M4 clearance holes → plate with a raised boss carrying three M3 tapped holes on a 20 mm bolt circle, exported as solid B-Rep STEP → full parametric probe enclosure.
  • Zoo worked like a real CAD platform, with manual editing after generation, a code view of the features, and more export options. Took about 20 minutes on the full enclosure prompt. Adam was easier to learn but had no manual editing and only exported STEP and SCAD. It couldn't handle the enclosure prompt.
  • Threaded and patterned features were the limit across all of them. Prismatic geometry generated fine; tapped holes on a bolt circle did not.

The architecture

The Duke 250/16 is a 16-bit, word-addressed, MIPS-like RISC architecture. I built a single-cycle implementation of it in Logisim from the given spec.

  • Instruction set: 16 instructions across R, I, and J formats: arithmetic (add, sub, addi), logic (and, not), shifts (sll, srl), memory (lw, sw), branches (beq, bgez), jumps (j, jr, jal), and I/O (input, output).
  • Registers: 8 general purpose. $r0 hardwired to zero, so it needs no storage. $r7 is the link register for jal.
  • Harvard architecture: separate instruction and data memory. ROM for instructions, RAM for data, both 16-bit address and data width.
  • 6-bit immediates, sign-extended to 16 bits.

What I built

  • Register file: read ports built with tri-state buffers and a decoder rather than a mux. Logically equivalent, but faster in a real implementation.
  • ALU: arithmetic, logic, and both shift operations.
  • Instruction decode: opcode to control signals driving the ALU operation, mux selects, and write enables.
  • Next PC logic: increment, branch offset, jump target, and register jump paths.
  • Datapath: the subcircuits wired together into a working processor, plus keyboard and TTY for the I/O instructions.

Timing and memory

  • Five clocked items total. Register file and TTY clock on the rising edge; PC register, data memory, and keyboard on the falling edge.
  • Logisim's RAM takes non-zero time to process data, so a memory_latch sits before it to latch address, data in, write enable, and output enable. Side effect is that sw lands a cycle later than expected.
  • Asynchronous reset clears the PC, register file, TTY, and keyboard buffer without touching memory.

Testing

  • Debugged in Logisim rather than guessing. Loaded the imem and dmem images, stepped the clock to the cycle before a failure, then traced the bad value back until it reached a subcircuit producing wrong output from right inputs.
  • Wrote assembly test programs and traced them against the reference simulator in verbose mode to know what each register should hold at each step.
  • Built up incrementally: ALU operations first, then memory, then branches and jumps.

The challenge

Five bots per lab section, each assigned a continent and a different sensor, together locating NAE Grand Challenge sites. Mine covered North America with an RFID reader, checking whether an active tag was present and where. The bots had to agree on a shared result and act on it together.

Navigation and sensing

  • Line following around the continent using QTI reflectance sensors driving servo motors, with threshold calibration so tracking held up as surface and lighting conditions changed.
  • Stopped at the northern and southern hash marks, took an RFID reading at each, and resolved it into a three-state score: 0 for no tag, 1 for a tag at the southern mark, 2 for the northern mark.

Communication

  • Transmitted the score over XBee and received the other four bots' results, holding the full team array and displaying it on an onboard LCD.
  • Status indication on two RGB LEDs with timing requirements. External LED green or red for sense outcome and blue or cyan for transmit and receive (≥250 ms), onboard LED red or green for which hash mark the bot had reached (≥500 ms). Useful for debugging where in the sequence a bot had stalled.

Team response

  • Summed the team's five scores and branched on the result: a light show below 5, a dance at 5 or above, and a song if every bot returned the same value.
  • Light show and dance had to stay synchronized across all five bots to within 0.25–0.5 s, and the song to within a note, which made XBee message timing and the shared start trigger the hard part rather than the individual routines.
